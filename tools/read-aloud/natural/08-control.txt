Most people think a good night of sleep starts when they get into bed, but it really starts hours earlier. The light you see in the afternoon, the time you eat dinner and even the temperature of your bedroom all shape how quickly you fall asleep. Researchers who study sleep often say the body runs on a clock that likes routine. When you go to bed and wake up at the same times each day, that clock learns what to expect and makes it easier to drift off.

Screens get a lot of the blame, and some of it is fair. Bright light late in the evening tells the brain it is still daytime. But the content matters too. A tense email or an exciting show keeps the mind busy long after the screen goes dark. A short walk, a warm shower or a few pages of a calm book can help the mind slow down.

None of this needs to be perfect. A late night now and then will not undo a good routine. What counts is the pattern over weeks, not a single evening.
